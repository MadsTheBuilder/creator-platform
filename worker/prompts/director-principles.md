# Director's principles

A breakdown that only describes what the script says is a transcription, not direction. These are the moves that turn a script into a film. Each one is described generally; use what fits the Vision Brief and skip what doesn't. Record every deliberate choice in Notes, with the shot numbers involved, so the crew understands the intent.

## 1. Find the spine before the shots
Before drafting any shot, write down in one line what the film is about underneath the plot (e.g. "a system that looks protective turns out to be blind"), what the audience should feel at the end, and the moment the whole piece turns on. Every scene then has a job: setup, escalation, reveal, release. A shot that does nothing for the spine gets cut or merged.

## 2. Plants and payoffs
Search the script for any later reveal that depends on an earlier detail: an object, a line, a costume element, a habit. Then **plant it visibly earlier** in its own shot (an ECU or INS) and write `PLANT: pays off in Shot N`. At the payoff shot, write `PAYOFF of Shot N`. If the script reveals something with no setup, invent the plant and say you did.

## 3. A visual motif
Give the film one recurring image or colour tied to its theme (an object, a colour that is the only saturated one in frame, a shape, a light source). Introduce it early, bring it back at turning points, and end on it. Write `MOTIF` in Notes when it appears.

## 4. Mirrors and rhymes
When the story returns to an event (a retelling, a flashback, a false version and then the truth, a before and after), **repeat the framing**: the same magnification, lens and angle, with only the meaning changed. When a character returns to a place or state, repeat their framing (`Same framing as Shots 12, 40`). The audience feels the repetition before they consciously notice it.

## 5. Withholding and reveals
Choose what the audience isn't allowed to see yet: a face, a location, an object, a reaction. Shoot around it (backs, silhouettes, out of focus, cropped frames) until the reveal shot, then give the reveal its own clean, held shot. Suspects in a mystery should be framed neutrally, with only a small hint the audience can recognise afterwards.

## 6. One graphics system
If the script uses recurring information (names and titles, timestamps, maps, data, chapter cards, product claims, prices), design **one template per type** and reuse it. The first use gets a full note describing the template; later uses say `Same template as Shot N`. Mark graphics-only shots with GFX.

## 7. Coverage rhythm
Vary the magnification on purpose: wide to establish, tighten as tension rises, go to an ECU on the beat that matters, then pull wide again for release or isolation. Avoid long runs of the same size. Comedy usually holds wider (the body is the joke) and cuts to a reaction; thrillers tighten; ads cut fast and land on the product.

## 8. Every spoken line gets a shot
Walk the script line by line. Every VO, dialogue or on-screen-text line must appear in some shot's Audio column, word for word, in order. Long narration can span several shots; mark where it continues with `(cont.)`. Silence is a choice too: write it down.

## 9. Runtime maths
Durations must add up to the target runtime (within ±10%). A rough guide: about 2.5 words per second of narration in English or Hinglish, and about 3 per second for fast ad VO. A shot is as long as its line plus breathing room, never shorter than its line. Fast-cut formats use 1–3s shots; contemplative docs use 4–8s; hero or establishing shots run up to 10–15s.

## 10. Notes the crew can act on
Notes should say *why* and *how*: grade or palette, emotional purpose, continuity warnings, practical issues (permits, child actors, night shoot, VFX), and what must be visible in frame. Avoid vague words like "cinematic" or "beautiful".

## 11. Flag problems in the script
If the script repeats itself, contradicts itself, has an impossible shot, a line with no speaker, or something that needs legal or ethical care (real names, minors, crime recreation, brand claims), make a decision, note it in the relevant shot, and list it in the final report. Don't silently fix it, and don't silently ignore it.

## 12. Light carries the emotion
Each scene gets a lighting setup derived from the Lighting bible and from that scene's emotion; each shot adapts it to its framing. Change the light at turning points so the audience feels the shift. See `lighting-design.md`.

## 13. Open and close with intent
The first shot sets scale, tone and promise. The last shot is the image the audience takes home, usually the motif or a mirror of the opening. Write both down deliberately.
